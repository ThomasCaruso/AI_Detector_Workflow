Northlake Logistics is a regional cold-chain distributor serving grocery stores and independent restaurants across three states. In the fictional 2025 fiscal year, the company generated $48.0 million of revenue and $7.2 million of EBITDA, implying a 15.0% EBITDA margin. Revenue grew 11% from the prior year, but management estimates that roughly four percentage points of that growth came from a temporary fuel-surcharge program rather than underlying shipment growth. The company ended the year with $9.5 million of debt and $1.5 million of cash.

A buyer is considering acquiring Northlake at an enterprise value of $50.4 million, equal to 7.0 times reported EBITDA. The headline multiple overstates the attractiveness of the deal if the fuel-surcharge contribution is treated as temporary. Management estimates that normalized EBITDA would have been $6.6 million without the unusually favorable surcharge contribution. On that basis, the purchase price represents about 7.6 times normalized EBITDA.

The investment case depends on three operating improvements. First, the buyer believes route-density software can reduce empty return miles from 18% of fleet miles to 14% within two years. Second, management plans to renegotiate two warehouse leases that together represent $1.1 million of annual rent; the underwriting assumes only $180,000 of annual savings, not the full amount management has discussed. Third, Northlake has lost several small customers because weekend delivery capacity is constrained. The plan assumes modest weekend expansion adds $2.5 million of revenue by year three at a 20% incremental EBITDA margin.

There are material risks. Northlake's largest customer represents 17% of revenue, and its contract expires eighteen months after the proposed acquisition. Driver turnover was 31% last year, above management's 24% target. The route-density savings also require reliable dispatch data, but approximately 12% of historical route records have missing or inconsistent timestamps. The underwriting therefore does not assume that all identified efficiency opportunities are achieved.

The base case assumes revenue reaches $56.0 million and EBITDA reaches $9.0 million by year five. It also assumes net debt falls to $4.0 million. If the company is sold at the same 7.0 times EBITDA multiple, the implied exit enterprise value would be $63.0 million. The purpose of the case is not to assert that the acquisition is attractive, but to test whether a rewriting system preserves the difference between reported and normalized earnings, the limited nature of the operating assumptions, and the stated concentration and execution risks.
