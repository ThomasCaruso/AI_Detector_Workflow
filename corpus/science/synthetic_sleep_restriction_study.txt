A hypothetical laboratory study examined whether five consecutive nights of moderate sleep restriction affected sustained attention in healthy adults. The study enrolled 120 participants between the ages of 21 and 35. Participants were randomly assigned to either a sleep-restriction group or a control group, with 60 people in each condition. The restriction group was given a five-hour sleep opportunity each night for five nights, while the control group was given an eight-hour sleep opportunity. The protocol did not attempt to model chronic insomnia, shift work, or sleep disorders.

Sustained attention was measured using a ten-minute computerized reaction-time task administered at baseline and again on the morning after the fifth experimental night. The primary outcome was the number of lapses, defined in the fictional protocol as responses slower than 500 milliseconds. At baseline, the restriction group averaged 3.1 lapses and the control group averaged 3.0 lapses. After the intervention, the restriction group averaged 8.4 lapses while the control group averaged 3.5. The mean increase was therefore 5.3 lapses in the restriction group and 0.5 lapses in the control group.

The fictional researchers also collected self-reported sleepiness on a ten-point scale. Average sleepiness increased from 3.2 to 6.8 in the restriction group and from 3.1 to 3.6 in the control group. However, the change in self-reported sleepiness was only moderately correlated with the change in attention lapses. The brief therefore does not claim that participants could accurately judge the size of their own performance decline.

Several limitations are intentionally important. The sample excluded people who reported major medical conditions, current night-shift work, or habitual sleep below six hours per night. The experiment lasted only five nights. Caffeine was prohibited during laboratory testing but was not experimentally manipulated as an independent variable. The reaction-time task measured one narrow dimension of attention rather than overall intelligence, productivity, or real-world safety.

The intended conclusion is correspondingly narrow: within this fictional experiment, five nights of a five-hour sleep opportunity were associated with a larger deterioration in sustained-attention performance than an eight-hour sleep opportunity. The document does not establish the size of the effect in the general population, does not show that every individual would respond similarly, and does not justify claims about long-term cognitive damage.
