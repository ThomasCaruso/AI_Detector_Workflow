# Structural Reconstruction

Use this reference when a rewrite still feels polished but templated, especially in deep-rewrite mode.

## Core principle

A deep rewrite should not be a source-order paraphrase. Once the semantic lock is built, draft from the lock and the argument itself rather than walking sentence by sentence through the source.

This is a writing-quality technique, not a license to alter meaning. Fidelity remains the hard constraint.

## 1. Reset the opening

Treat the source opening as disposable unless it contains immutable language or is clearly the best entry point.

Ask which opening best serves the material:

- the most consequential implication;
- a concrete example that exposes the mechanism;
- a contrast that creates the central tension;
- a surprising constraint;
- the claim itself, if directness is genuinely strongest.

Do not preserve a source opening merely because it is already fluent.

## 2. Rebuild the support order

List the required claims, then choose their order from scratch.

Do not automatically preserve an essay-shaped sequence such as:

1. thesis;
2. first reason;
3. second reason;
4. third reason;
5. counterpoint;
6. balanced conclusion.

Possible alternatives include:

- example → mechanism → broader claim → limitation;
- constraint → consequence → evidence → judgment;
- strongest mechanism → counterpoint → secondary mechanism → implication;
- opposing case → why it fails → main claim;
- consequence first, explanation second.

The order should be defensible from the content, not arbitrary.

## 3. Use asymmetric emphasis

Do not give every requested subpoint equal space merely because each appeared in the prompt.

Preserve every required point, but let the most important or difficult mechanism receive more development. Secondary points may be compressed into clauses, short sentences, or supporting examples.

Equal paragraph or sentence allocation often creates a visible template even when each sentence is individually good.

## 4. Allow sentences to do more than one job

A sentence may contain evidence plus judgment, mechanism plus qualification, or contrast plus consequence when those ideas naturally belong together.

Avoid constructing a sequence where every sentence performs exactly one rhetorical function and then hands off cleanly to the next.

Do not make sentences convoluted merely to combine functions. Clarity still wins.

## 5. Reduce transition scaffolding

Transitions are useful when the relationship would otherwise be unclear. They are not required at the start of every new rhetorical move.

During revision, inspect sequences such as:

- `This also...`
- `That pressure...`
- `Therefore...`
- `In addition...`
- `The same dynamic...`
- `However...`
- `Even so...`
- `Ultimately...`

If the relationship is already obvious from adjacency, delete the transition or rewrite the sentence so the connection is carried by content rather than a signpost.

## 6. Watch polished balance

Repeated contrast frames, mirrored clauses, three-part enumerations, and perfectly resolved antitheses can create a highly engineered cadence when they occur repeatedly.

Keep a balanced construction when it is the sharpest way to express the idea. Do not stack many of them in the same passage.

During the architecture audit, flag patterns such as:

- `X can do A; Y cannot.`
- `not because X, but because Y` repeated more than once;
- multiple `A, B, or C` lists;
- repeated colon-led summaries;
- several sentences with matching grammatical shape.

The fix is not random variation. Re-express the underlying reasoning in the form it naturally requires.

## 7. End where the argument actually lands

Do not assume the last sentence must restate the thesis in cleaner language.

Possible endings:

- the main implication;
- the unresolved constraint;
- the strongest counterweight;
- the consequence of the mechanism;
- a concrete condition under which the claim holds.

If the thesis has already been established, another full summary can weaken the piece.

## 8. Architecture audit

After the global revision, inspect the document as a sequence of rhetorical functions.

Write the sequence internally in shorthand, for example:

`claim → reason A → reason B → reason C → caveat → thesis restatement`

If the sequence looks like a generic essay template rather than the natural shape of the subject, rebuild it.

Also check:

- Did the rewrite reuse the source opening without a substantive reason?
- Did it preserve the source's sentence order too closely?
- Are transitions doing work that sentence adjacency could do?
- Are several sentences built from the same contrast/list pattern?
- Did every subpoint receive suspiciously equal space?
- Does the final sentence merely summarize what is already obvious?
- Did restructuring alter any fact, causal relationship, or level of certainty?

Only the final question overrides structural experimentation: fidelity is mandatory.
