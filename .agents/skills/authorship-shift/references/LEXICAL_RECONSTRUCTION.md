# Lexical Reconstruction

Use this reference when the document structure is sound but the wording still feels generic, over-smoothed, or drawn from familiar analytical phrase families.

## Core principle

Do not try to make prose "unpredictable" by using obscure synonyms, deliberate mistakes, slang, or random variation. Instead make lexical choices follow the subject more closely.

A strong sentence should contain more language that belongs to the actual mechanism being discussed and less language whose only job is to sound like polished analysis.

## 1. Remove abstract wrappers

Weak analytical prose often wraps a simple point in a generic frame:

- `This can also change how companies think about risk.`
- `The result can be faster problem solving.`
- `This creates an incentive to improve.`
- `That pressure turns improvement into a necessity.`
- `Cost reduction works the same way.`

When possible, state the mechanism directly instead of naming the rhetorical function around it.

For example, prefer a sentence that says what managers fund, what engineers change, what customers compare, or what a rival can exploit.

## 2. Prefer content-bearing verbs

Generic prose overuses verbs such as:

- make;
- create;
- drive;
- enable;
- allow;
- lead to;
- result in;
- change;
- improve;
- impact.

These words are not forbidden. Replace them only when the subject supplies a more exact action.

Examples of stronger choices depend on the content: `cut`, `delay`, `fund`, `ship`, `copy`, `switch`, `compress`, `underprice`, `redesign`, `scrap`, `license`, `standardize`, `queue`, `retry`, or `defer` may carry more information than a generic wrapper.

## 3. Prefer domain nouns over analytical nouns

Watch repeated abstractions such as:

- pressure;
- dynamic;
- factor;
- process;
- approach;
- outcome;
- advantage;
- improvement;
- innovation;
- efficiency.

Keep them when they name the actual concept. Otherwise ask whether the sentence can name the concrete object: price, chip, battery cell, launch pad, labor hour, API timeout, margin, supplier, research budget, or customer.

## 4. Audit stock phrase families

During revision, flag familiar connective or argumentative frames when several appear in one passage:

- `X works the same way`;
- `X can also...`;
- `The result can be...`;
- `A useful example is...` / `X is a useful example`;
- `None of this means...`;
- `This does not mean...`;
- `Even with those limits...`;
- `At the same time...`;
- `In a protected market... In a contested market...`;
- `not only X, but Y`;
- `not because X, but because Y`;
- `the key is...`;
- `what matters is...`.

Do not mechanically ban them. If a phrase is doing little more than organizing the essay, rewrite the sentence around the underlying fact or causal relationship.

## 5. Do not synonym-spin

Changing `important` to `significant`, `shows` to `demonstrates`, or `use` to `utilize` usually preserves the same generic sentence underneath.

When a sentence feels templated, change one of these instead:

- the subject;
- the predicate;
- the information order;
- the level of abstraction;
- which fact carries the sentence;
- whether the sentence needs a transition at all.

Lexical reconstruction is therefore structural at the sentence level, not a thesaurus pass.

## 6. Draft from lexical anchors

For each major idea, identify two or three words that come directly from the content and should carry the sentence.

Example anchors for a competition paragraph might include:

- `customers switch`;
- `rival ships`;
- `margin`;
- `research budget`;
- `reusable rocket`;
- `launch turnaround`.

Build the sentence around those anchors rather than around a generic frame such as `competition creates pressure`.

## 7. Use internal alternatives when a sentence is generic

If a major sentence could appear unchanged in dozens of unrelated essays, silently produce at least two materially different renderings before choosing one.

Prefer the rendering with:

- more content-bearing nouns and verbs;
- fewer generic connective phrases;
- less duplicated syntax from nearby sentences;
- no loss of clarity, precision, or epistemic fidelity.

Do not choose a stranger word merely because it is rarer.

## 8. Preserve ordinary vocabulary

Natural writing is often simple. The goal is not maximal lexical diversity.

Repetition is correct when the same technical concept is being discussed. Common words are correct when they are the clearest words. A sentence can be plain and still be highly specific.

## 9. Lexical audit

Before returning a deep rewrite, ask internally:

- Which phrases could be pasted into an unrelated business or technology essay with almost no change?
- Which verbs name only a generic relationship instead of the real action?
- Which nouns are abstractions standing in for concrete objects already available in the source?
- Did several sentences begin with rhetorical scaffolding rather than content?
- Did I use mirrored contrasts or polished antitheses because they were convenient rather than necessary?
- Can any sentence be rebuilt around a domain-specific noun or verb without changing meaning?

Revise the worst offenders. Do not force every sentence to become unusual.

## Boundary

This method exists to improve specificity, voice, and writing quality. It should not be used to promise or target a particular result from an authorship or AI-detection system.
